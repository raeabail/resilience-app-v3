How the Resilience Scorecard Works

Understand the framework, build a landscape-specific assessment, and translate spatial data into actionable resilience priorities.

Framework

Our Approach to Resilience

A flexible framework for understanding how landscapes absorb, adapt to, and respond to environmental stress while maintaining essential functions

Resilience is the capacity of a landscape to absorb environmental stress, adapt to changing conditions, maintain essential functions, and recover from disturbance. It is not about preventing change, but about enabling systems to respond dynamically while preserving the ecological and social processes that communities depend on.

The Resilience Scorecard evaluates environmental hazards that are relevant to the landscape being assessed. The framework currently supports six hazard types: drought, extreme heat, flooding, land degradation, severe storms, and wildfire. Because resilience looks different across landscapes, the framework can be adapted using locally relevant hazards, indicators, datasets, and priorities.

Assessments can also be conducted at different spatial scales. Parcels are commonly used where property-level conservation decisions are important, but the framework can be applied to any appropriate user-defined boundary layer.

Building Your Assessment

Follow six core steps to build a landscape-specific resilience assessment.

1

Define Your Assessment

Start with the landscape, decision-making goals, and spatial units you want to evaluate.

2

Select Relevant Hazards

Determine which environmental hazards are most important to your landscape and the decisions the assessment will support.

3

Identify Resilience Indicators

Identify the landscape characteristics and processes that influence resilience to each hazard, then select spatial datasets to represent them.

4

Build Hazard Assessments

Analyze the selected indicators and standardize results to a consistent 1–5 scale to assess resilience to each hazard.

5

Integrate Hazard Scores

Combine individual hazard assessments using an appropriate weighting approach to reveal overall patterns of resilience and vulnerability.

6

Translate Results into Action

Use the results to identify resilience anchors, vulnerabilities, restoration opportunities, and areas where conservation or management could strengthen resilience.

Understanding the Framework

Learn how hazard-specific resilience is evaluated and translated into comparable scores.

Hazards

Hazard-Specific Resilience

Not all hazards apply equally across landscapes. Select those most relevant to your assessment and expand each to understand the stress being evaluated and the landscape characteristics that can strengthen resilience.

Drought

Drought is a prolonged period of reduced water availability that can stress ecosystems, water resources, and land uses. Resilient landscapes are better able to retain and access water through characteristics such as soil water capacity, groundwater availability, healthy vegetation, and functioning hydrologic systems.

Extreme Heat

Extreme heat occurs when unusually high temperatures place stress on ecosystems, communities, and infrastructure. Resilient landscapes can moderate temperature extremes through vegetation cover, soil moisture, access to water, and other landscape characteristics that support cooler and more stable local conditions.

Flooding

Flooding occurs when water accumulates or moves across areas beyond their normal capacity. Resilient landscapes can store, slow, and safely convey excess water through functioning floodplains, wetlands, permeable soils, riparian areas, and natural drainage networks.

Land Degradation

Land degradation is the decline of ecological condition or land function through processes such as erosion, vegetation loss, fragmentation, disturbance, or land conversion. Resilient landscapes maintain soil stability, vegetation cover, habitat function, and ecological integrity despite ongoing pressures.

Severe Storms

Severe storms can expose landscapes to intense rainfall, high winds, damaging weather, and associated disturbance. Resilience depends on the ability of natural and managed systems to absorb these impacts, maintain function during disturbance, and recover afterward.

Wildfire

Wildfire can alter vegetation, habitat, soils, infrastructure, and other landscape functions. Resilient landscapes reduce vulnerability through characteristics such as appropriate vegetation structure, manageable fuel conditions, lower-risk land cover patterns, and the capacity to recover following fire.

Process

Scoring Methodology

To translate landscape characteristics into comparable resilience scores, the framework follows a consistent analytical process:

  1. Identify the indicators and metrics that influence resilience to each selected hazard.
  2. Evaluate each spatial unit using consistent, spatially explicit datasets and standardize results to a common scoring scale.
  3. Combine indicator scores within each hazard, then integrate hazard results using weights or contextual adjustments appropriate to the landscape and assessment objectives.

Results are standardized on a 1–5 scale, with 1 indicating very low resilience and 5 indicating very high resilience.

Technical Details

Spatial inputs are prepared to a consistent coordinate system and analysis environment before metrics are evaluated. Outputs include hazard-specific scores, composite resilience results, spatial layers, and supporting tables that can be used for mapping, comparison, prioritization, and reporting.
